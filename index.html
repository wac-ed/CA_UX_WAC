The Context-Aware UX Framework (CAUX)

This paper proposes the Context-Aware UX Framework (CAUX): an intent-driven approach to e-commerce design. The core idea: successful e-commerce comes from delivering the right experience at the right stage of the customer’s journey, not from adding more features. The seven journey stages along the bottom recur throughout the deck.

01 · The challenge

Feature-rich interfaces show too much, too soon

Cognitive load

Banners, competing CTAs and menus all draw on the same limited attention.

Decision fatigue

Every unnecessary choice drains the energy users need to commit.

Friction

Distractions interrupt decisions and push shoppers toward abandonment.

The problem is rarely missing functionality. It is presenting functionality at the right time.

E-commerce has evolved into experience-driven ecosystems where UX decides acquisition, conversion and retention. Despite investment in personalization, AI and commerce tech, many platforms still fail at the basics: helping users complete goals with clarity, confidence and minimal effort. Interfaces overloaded with promotional banners, competing calls-to-action, excessive navigation and features that demand attention regardless of relevance raise cognitive load, create decision fatigue, and reduce conversion.

02 · The research gap

Known principles, applied in isolation

Well established in HCI, cognitive psychology and usability engineering:

What

Existing research gives us well-established principles, but they are usually applied one at a time. Few studies explain what to present, when, and how interface priorities should shift as intent changes. Most design systems define consistency through reusable components, but few ask whether those components should stay equally visible at every stage. CAUX is proposed to fill that gap.

The question that changes everything

“What features should this page contain?”

“What does the user need at this moment to confidently move to the next step?”

This is the shift at the heart of the paper. Effective e-commerce UX should be designed around user intent, not interface pages. A homepage visitor seeks orientation and discovery; a customer in checkout wants to finish with as little friction as possible. Treating both moments with the same priorities adds distraction and raises abandonment. The goal is not to reduce functionality but to make every element serve a clear purpose in the user’s current context.

03 · The evolution of e-commerce UX

From catalogs to clarity

STAGE 1

Transaction-centric

Digital catalogs. If users could find it and order it, the site worked.

STAGE 2

Experience-centric

Smarter search, better recommendations, simpler checkout. UX becomes a business driver.

STAGE 3

Feature overload

Flash sales, loyalty, AI assistants, wishlists, social proof, all at once.

THE NEXT STEP

Clarity over complexity

Focused interactions that match each user’s current goal.

UX now moves metrics:

navigation → discovery · product pages → confidence · checkout → lower abandonment

Early online retail won on availability, price and the ability to transact. As competition intensified and switching became easy, experience became the differentiator: the shift from transaction-centric to experience-centric commerce. Today UX influences nearly every critical metric, and poor UX can cancel out investments in marketing, pricing or product quality. But in chasing engagement, platforms keep adding features. Each is valuable alone; shown together they cause overload. More functionality does not automatically create a better experience. The best platforms prioritise clarity over complexity.

Brand-owned stores sell their own products and aim to strengthen brand perception; visitors often arrive already interested. Marketplaces aggregate thousands of sellers; users want the most suitable product as efficiently as possible, so storytelling is secondary. Multi-brand retailers sit in between: they own inventory, pricing, fulfilment and service, and must balance efficient discovery with retailer credibility and online-to-offline experiences. D2C brands cut out intermediaries and use their sites to build identity and long-term relationships. Luxury sites reinforce exclusivity rather than maximise immediate conversion; marketplace layouts or aggressive promotions would undermine them. B2B platforms prioritise efficiency, data accuracy, account-based pricing and repeat ordering over visual storytelling.

Strategy and intent first, design trends last

1

2

3

Designers often borrow patterns from successful products without considering the context they were created for, producing experiences that look modern but are strategically misaligned. Adding extensive comparison features to a luxury fashion site distracts from its emotional narrative; replacing robust filtering on a marketplace with immersive storytelling makes discovery harder. These three questions are the foundation of the framework.

05 · The psychology of online shopping

Seven truths about how people shop

01 Users scan

They look for what’s sold, whether to trust it and where to start.

02 Load costs

Working memory is limited; every element demands processing.

03 Choice tires

Category, product, brand, colour, size, delivery, payment: each drains energy.

04 Trust builds

Credibility is judged from the first second, not just at payment.

05 Recognise

Beats recall: recently viewed, saved filters, persistent carts, history.

06 Momentum

Each step builds toward the next; interruptions reset it.

07 Emotion

Reviews, delivery dates and return policies reassure before money is spent.

Users want their goal with the least mental effort.

1. Users scan rather than read; on landing they ask what the site sells, whether they can trust it, where to start, and whether they can find things quickly. Clarity and visual hierarchy answer these instantly. 2. Every decision has a cost: working memory is limited. 3. Decision fatigue: a checkout page that pushes browsing, loyalty sign-up, coupons and blog posts forces users to reconsider a decision already made. 4. Trust accumulates through professional design, transparent pricing, authentic reviews, delivery information, support and consistency, so users arrive at payment already convinced. 5. Recognition is easier than recall, so make information visible when needed. 6. Momentum: discovery leads to comparison, evaluation, confidence and purchase. 7. Emotion: a confusing error frustrates, a slow page creates impatience, an unexpected shipping charge disappoints; clear confirmations and transparent pricing build confidence. The challenge is deciding when features should be visible, not which features to have.

06 · The Context-Aware UX Framework · Core principle

Every interface element should justify its presence by helping the user achieve their immediate goal.

CAUX doesn’t remove features. It presents them when they are most relevant.

Most interfaces are static: the same navigation, banners, recommendation widgets, wishlists and campaigns persist on every page. CAUX designs around user intent instead of pages, features or business departments. Example: a wishlist is valuable, but a customer entering payment probably doesn’t need it. A weekend-sale banner may work on the homepage, but at checkout it introduces an alternative decision at the exact moment the user has committed.

06 · The shift

From feature-centric to intent-centric

Feature-centric

“What features should we include?”

Every team competes for visibility

Same banners and widgets on every page

A static interface

Intent-centric

“What does the user need right now?”

One objective per moment

Irrelevant features postponed, not deleted

An interface that evolves with intent

Digital products grow by adding functionality, and over time every team competes for visibility: marketing wants promotional banners, merchandising wants featured products, loyalty teams want reward programmes, support wants chat widgets. The result is a homepage where everything claims equal importance. Users arrive with a single objective, such as finding a laptop, checking delivery or paying. Intent-centric design asks whether a feature contributes to the current task; if not, its visibility is reduced or postponed.

06 · The framework

The seven principles of CAUX

01

Goal-First Design

One dominant purpose per page; secondary actions never compete.

02

Contextual Feature Exposure

Feature visibility changes as intent changes.

03

Progressive Disclosure

Information unfolds as interest deepens.

04

Decision Momentum

Protect forward progress in high-intent stages.

05

Visual Priority Hierarchy

Show importance through hierarchy, not volume.

06

Checkout Isolation

Remove distractions from a task users chose to finish.

07

Trust Accumulation

Confidence grows at every stage, not only at payment.

No new components. A new way to prioritise them.

1 Goal-First: homepage orients, listing compares, product page reduces uncertainty, checkout completes; competing objectives are friction. 2 Contextual Feature Exposure: features appear when relevant (see next slide). 3 Progressive Disclosure: a product card needs only image, name, price, rating; specs, warranty and reviews come once the user opens the product. 4 Decision Momentum: after comparing, choosing a variant and adding to cart, don’t pull users back out. 5 Visual Priority Hierarchy: distinguish primary actions, supporting information, secondary actions and optional functionality. 6 Checkout Isolation: minimise global navigation, banners, category links and unrelated recommendations. 7 Trust Accumulation: trust is the cumulative outcome of hundreds of design decisions. CAUX changes how existing components are prioritised: the experience becomes an evolving conversation between user and interface.

06 · Applying the framework

The CAUX decision test

Does it help the current goal?

Does it reduce cognitive effort?

Does it build trust at this stage?

None of the three?

Reconsider its visibility. Relevance over quantity, purpose over presence.

Every interface element is evaluated with three questions: Does this help the user accomplish their current goal? Does this reduce cognitive effort rather than increase it? Does this improve confidence or trust at this stage of the journey? If an element satisfies none, its visibility should be reconsidered.

07 · The modern shopping journey

Users think in goals, not pages

StageThe user is askingThe interface should
1 Awareness“Am I in the right place?”Orient and build clarity
2 Exploration“What options do I have?”Make finding products fast
3 Evaluation“Which option best fits my needs?”Remove uncertainty
4 Decision“Is this the right choice to buy now?”Reassure; remove final barriers
5 Purchase“How quickly can I complete this?”Speed and checkout isolation
6 Post-purchase“Was my order successful?”Confirm, then track delivery
7 Loyalty“How do I manage this relationship?”Personalise; make reordering easy
Awareness lasts seconds: the homepage should answer what the business offers, how to find things and why to trust it. Exploration: intuitive categories, responsive search, meaningful filters; narrow thousands of options into a manageable set. Evaluation: shift from helping users find products to helping them understand them; specs, imagery, authentic reviews, delivery, warranty, returns and comparison, ordered by user priorities. Decision: transparent pricing, shipping, secure payment messaging and clear returns; extra alternatives reopen resolved decisions. Purchase: simplified forms, progress indicators, guest checkout, inline validation, transparent pricing and security messaging. Post-purchase: confirmation, delivery estimate, tracking, support and next steps before suggesting more shopping. Loyalty: returning customers want reorders, tracking, account updates; personalisation beats broad promotions, and relationships are built through effortless interactions, not discounts alone.

Homepage: often treated as prime marketing space where departments compete; for users it is about orientation. Navigation: customers think in products and problems, not departments; good navigation is barely noticed. Search: search users have stronger purchase intent; search should recognise synonyms, suggest, remember past searches and recover gracefully, acting as a guide rather than a filter. Listing pages: consistent cards with only what initial comparison needs; avoid excess badges and competing CTAs. Product detail: the deciding moment; prioritise confidence over persuasion, eliminate uncertainty. Cart: reinforce commitment with summaries, transparent pricing, shipping estimates, coupons and quantity controls. Checkout: remove every obstacle between intention and completion. Post-purchase: confirmation, order details, delivery dates and tracking come before recommendations, referrals or loyalty offers.

Effective UX is rarely one major innovation. Individually these decisions seem insignificant; collectively they decide whether an experience feels intuitive or frustrating. The difference between an average and an exceptional platform is usually consistent execution of small details. As refinements accumulate, they reduce friction, strengthen trust and raise goal completion, which shows up as better conversion, retention, satisfaction and loyalty. Exceptional e-commerce is built through intentional prioritisation, not feature accumulation.

09 · Regulation in India · E-Commerce Amendment Rules 2026

E-commerce rules, touchpoint by touchpoint

Search and listings

No manipulated results. Sponsored items clearly labelled. Main ranking factors explained in plain language.

Prices and fees

Show the prior price (lowest in 30 days) beside any discount. No bundled fees for unrelated services.

Product pages

Best-before dates, returns, refunds, warranty, delivery and return-shipping costs, payment modes.

Seller and origin

Seller ID (e.g. GSTIN), website and email; importer and country of origin. Seller name as large as ours on invoices.

Customer data

Express, affirmative consent before using customer data to promote own-brand or related sellers.

Trust and grievances

Yearly dark-pattern audit, certificate on display. Complaint copy to user; acknowledge in 48 h, resolve in a month; join NCH.

In force from 1 Jan 2027. Country-of-origin filter from 1 Jul 2027. A design summary, not legal advice.

Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Amendment Rules 2026, notified 9 September 2026, in force 1 January 2027 (not applicable to this year’s festive sales). Search: platforms may not manipulate search results or indexes in a way that misleads users or reduces relevance; sponsored listings need clear, prominent disclosure; marketplaces must explain their main ranking parameters and their relative importance in plain language. Prices: when a price reduction is announced, show the reduced price and the prior price, defined as the lowest price in the preceding 30 days. Fees: marketplaces may not collect bundled fees for services unrelated to the platform; loyalty and membership programmes are exempt. Product and seller disclosures: best-before or use-before dates; return, refund, exchange, warranty and guarantee terms; delivery, shipping and return-shipping costs; accepted payment modes; seller government IDs such as GSTIN or MSME registration; seller website and email where available; importer details and the full country-of-origin name for imported goods. Seller name on the invoice in the same font size as the platform name. Customer data: marketplaces need express and affirmative consent before using consumer information for specified purposes, such as promoting related-party or same-brand sellers. Trust: comply with the 2023 Dark Patterns Guidelines, self-audit every year and display the certificate prominently. Grievances: give complainants a copy of the complaint as recorded by the grievance officer; the 48-hour acknowledgement and one-month redressal timelines remain; join the National Consumer Helpline (NCH) convergence process. The searchable country-of-origin filter comes from the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules and applies from 1 July 2027.

09 · Regulation in India · DPDP Act 2023 and Rules 2025

Data protection, built into our flows

Privacy notice

Standalone and plain: what data, for what purpose, how to withdraw, use rights or complain.

Consent

Specific and given by clear action, so no pre-ticked boxes. Withdrawing as easy as giving.

Under-18s

Verifiable parental consent, with the parent’s age checked. No tracking or targeted ads.

Erasure

Delete data once its purpose is served; warn users 48 hours before. Keep logs for a year.

Breaches

Tell affected users promptly; detailed report to the Data Protection Board within 72 hours.

Rights and grievances

Publish a contact person; answer grievances within 90 days. Penalties up to ₹250 crore.

Consent managers from 13 Nov 2026; most obligations from May 2027. A design summary, not legal advice.

Digital Personal Data Protection Rules 2025, notified 13 November 2025 under the DPDP Act 2023. Phasing: Rules 1, 2 and 17–21 in force from notification; consent managers (Rule 4) from 13 November 2026; notice, security safeguards, breach handling, retention and children’s data (Rules 3, 5–16, 22–23) 18 months after notification, in May 2027 (sources differ between 13 and 14 May; confirm with legal). Notice (Rule 3): clear, independent and accessible; lists the personal data and specific purposes; explains how to withdraw consent, exercise rights and complain. Consent (Act s.6): free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given by clear affirmative action; withdrawal must be comparable in ease to giving consent. Children (Act s.9, Rule 10): verifiable parental consent, confirming the parent is an adult using reliable identity or age details or a virtual token such as DigiLocker; no tracking, behavioural monitoring or targeted advertising directed at children. Erasure: delete data once the specified purpose is no longer served unless law requires retention; notify the user 48 hours before erasure; retain personal data and logs for at least one year. Breaches: inform affected users promptly with details and mitigation steps; give the Data Protection Board an initial intimation and a detailed report within 72 hours. Rights (Rule 9 onwards): publish the DPO or contact person’s details; give clear instructions for exercising rights; respond to grievances within 90 days. Security safeguards include encryption, masking or tokenisation, access controls, logging and backups. Penalty for failing to maintain reasonable security safeguards: up to ₹250 crore. Note: “reject as easy as accept” on cookie banners is an industry reading of these rules, not their wording.

The Central Consumer Protection Authority’s Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023 list 13 specified dark patterns: false urgency, basket sneaking, confirm shaming, forced action, subscription trap, interface interference, bait and switch, drip pricing, disguised advertisement, nagging, trick question, SaaS billing and rogue malware. UX can’t be standardised

Start from the business model and user intent.

AI, conversational, immersive and predictive tools will change. Clarity, confidence and respect for attention will not.

As products become easy to replicate and pricing transparent, experience is often the deciding factor. Exposing every feature throughout the journey assumes visibility drives engagement, but it usually raises cognitive load and distracts. CAUX shifts focus from feature-centric to intent-centric design: relevance over quantity, clarity over complexity, guidance over distraction. A marketplace, a luxury brand, a D2C business and a B2B platform serve different audiences; what improves one can weaken another. Good design is contextual.
